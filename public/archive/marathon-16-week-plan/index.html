What this plan is honest about

Read this once, then don't think about it again

This plan gets you to the marathon start line genuinely fit, and it puts you on the sub-3 track rather than the just-finish track. That's a real distinction. A typical first-marathon plan peaks at 45 km a week with no pace work and produces someone who survives 42 km. This one peaks at 66, trains marathon pace directly, and produces someone who races it.

What it will not do is deliver sub-3. Sub-3 is 4:16 per kilometre held for 42.2 km, and the people who run it are training 70–100 km a week for years, not one block. Your realistic finish here depends almost entirely on the week-8 10K test, and the plan tells you how to convert that into a target when you have it.

Treat this as marathon number one of three or four. The sub-3 attempt is the one at the end of that sequence, and the fastest honest route to it runs straight through this block.

The one hard gate

Week 1 opens at 34 km across five runs with a 10 km long run. That is not a beginner's week one — it is week one of a plan that assumes you already run. Before you start, you need to be able to run 8 km continuously at conversational effort, and to have run 20–25 km a week for at least three consecutive weeks without pain.

If that's you, start at week 1. If it isn't, the four-week on-ramp below gets you there, and skipping it is the single most reliable way to turn this into a stress fracture instead of a marathon.

Converting the week-8 10K into a target

Take your 10K time and multiply by 4.7. A 45:00 10K predicts 3:32; a 40:00 predicts 3:08.

Then add 5–10% back on, because that formula assumes marathon-specific endurance you won't have yet in your first block. A 45:00 10K realistically means 3:40–3:55 on the day. Set your MP from the realistic number, not the predicted one. Training at a pace your endurance can't support is how first marathons blow up at 32 km.

Keep the effort check — it costs nothing

The pace band tells you what to run. The talk test tells you whether today is a day where that band is honest. On easy runs you should be able to say a full sentence out loud; if you can't, the pace is wrong for today regardless of what the plan says — heat, sleep and accumulated fatigue all move it. Distance is non-negotiable, pace is a target, effort is the veto.

The Thursday medium-long run is the most important addition here. It's Pfitzinger's signature session and it's what makes this a marathon plan rather than a long-run-plus-filler plan. It builds mitochondrial density and capillary growth without the recovery cost of a second long run, and it's the main reason this block peaks at 66 km instead of 45.

The week opening with a run means four run days in a row — Saturday long, Sunday recovery, Monday easy, Tuesday quality. That's deliberate and safe, because Sunday and Monday are the two easiest runs of the week, and the mid-week rest now lands on Wednesday, straight after the quality day, which is exactly where you want it.

One quality day, four easy days. That ratio is Patrick Sang's, and it's not up for improvement. The failure mode of every ambitious amateur is that the easy days creep up to medium and the hard day gets softer, until you're running five identical medium runs and plateauing for years.

The three-hour cap

Long runs are given in km, but stop at 3 hours whatever the distance says. Maximum aerobic adaptation from a long run lands around 2.5–3 hours; past that you add recovery cost and injury risk without much return. If 30 km takes you longer than 3 hours, run 3 hours. The trap

Once Tuesday gets hard, Wednesday and Thursday want to creep up too — you feel fit, and easy starts to feel insulting. Four easy runs and one hard one beats five medium ones by a distance. Elite Kenyan marathoners run roughly 80% of their kilometres below marathon pace. The analysis of 119,000 marathon runners found easy-pace volume is the single strongest thing separating fast marathoners from average ones — the fastest run ~80% easy, everyone else 50–60%.

Fuel

The part that decides the last 10 km

  • Under 60 minutes: water only.
  • Over 75 minutes: start fuelling. Begin at 30–40 g of carbohydrate per hour from week 5, taking the first one at 30 minutes rather than when you feel empty.
  • From week 9: build toward 60–90 g/hour on every long run. Use a 2:1 glucose-to-fructose product — glucose transport saturates around 60 g/h, and fructose opens a second pathway that gets you past it.
  • The gut is trainable and it needs training. Untrained, most people tolerate about 60 g/h. Two weeks of deliberate practice measurably improves absorption and reduces stomach trouble. The best predictor of race-day GI problems is not having rehearsed.
  • Fluid: 400–800 ml/hour with 300–600 mg sodium per hour, more in heat.
  • Before long runs: 1–2 g carbohydrate per kg bodyweight two hours out. At 70 kg that's 70–140 g.
  • Use exactly what the race hands out, or carry your own for the whole race. Race day is not the day to discover a new gel.
Heat, and where you live

August to October, default to the treadmill at 0.5–1% incline or run before 5:30am. Heat inflates effort at any given pace — the same speed that's easy in the gym is threshold outdoors in August. Trust the effort and let the pace collapse; the distance still counts. From November, move the long run and all MP work outdoors. Your race isn't on a treadmill, and pacing on a belt teaches you nothing about pacing yourself.

When it doesn't go to plan

It won't, and that's accounted for

  1. Missed one run. Let it go. Don't make it up, don't add it to another day.
  2. Missed most of a week. Repeat that week. Don't jump ahead to stay on schedule.
  3. Missed two weeks or more. Drop back to the start of the current block and rebuild.
  4. A week feels genuinely too hard — not unpleasant, but leaving you flat for days. Repeat it at 80% of the listed distances before moving on.
  5. Forced to choose which run to protect: long run first, medium-long second, quality last. Volume is what makes the marathon; the Tuesday session is what makes it fast, and fast is the thing you can afford to lose.
Stop the session

Sharp or pinching pain anywhere. Pain that grows as you run. Anything that changes how you move. Chest pain or dizziness. Pain above roughly 3 out of 10. Anything still present 48 hours later means you go back a week — not forward with a better attitude.

At 66 km a week the tissues that fail first are the calf, the Achilles, the shin and the plantar fascia, roughly in that order. Bone stress injuries announce themselves as a small point of pain that gets worse during a run rather than warming up. That one is not negotiable — stop and get it imaged.

Carry on

A dull ache that warms up and clears within ten minutes and is gone the next morning. Heavy legs on a Sunday after a Saturday long run. General fatigue in weeks 11 and 13. Being bored on easy runs — that means you're doing them right.

After this

Where the sub-3 actually lives

Finish this block and you'll have done something most people who talk about marathons never do, and you'll have a real marathon time to build on. The route from there:

  • Marathon 2 — the same shape, peaking at 80–85 km with two quality days instead of one. Aim to take 15–25 minutes off.
  • Marathon 3 — Pfitzinger 18/70 territory, 6 days a week, 90–100 km peak. This is the block where sub-3 becomes a live question rather than an aspiration.

The markers to watch on the way: 10K under 39 minutes, half marathon under 1:25, and 70–100 km a week sustained for twelve weeks or more. Hit those three and sub-3 is a matter of picking a flat race on a cool day. Miss them and no plan on earth delivers it.

The plan is aggressive on purpose. It peaks 45% higher than the standard first-marathon plan and it trains race pace directly, because you asked for something challenging and because the softer version would leave you on the wrong track for what you actually want.

The cost of that is that it has no slack in it. There's no week where you can be casual and catch up later. The rules about missed weeks aren't there for other people.
