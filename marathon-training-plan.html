Why this got rewritten

The fourth version, and the first one built on a current number

Every previous version of this plan was calibrated off a 27:30 5K. That number is out. 7.2 km/h for 20 minutes with heavy breathing is the only current measurement you've given me, and it describes someone with a genuinely low aerobic base right now — not a lapsed 27-minute 5K runner.

7.2 km/h is 8:20 per kilometre. If that's hard, then every pace zone in the last three versions was too fast, the volumes were too high, and following them would have hurt you inside a month. Training at paces you can't sustain doesn't make you tougher. It makes you injured, and then it makes you stop.

So here's the honest picture, and then a plan that actually fits it.

What changes about the goal

5:00/km inside 24 weeks is off the table. Not a maybe, not an outside chance. Going from "8:20/km is hard for 20 minutes" to "5:00/km for 42 kilometres" in six months isn't a training problem, it's an arithmetic one.

What's genuinely available to you in 24 weeks is a half marathon and an aerobic engine that doesn't currently exist. That's not a consolation prize. It's the thing every fast marathon is built on, and skipping it is why most people who chase a time goal from your starting point end up injured by month three with nothing to show.

The part that's actually in your favour

You're 24 and starting from close to zero, which means the next twelve months will produce the largest percentage gains of your entire running life. Beginners improve fastest — it's the one advantage that disappears permanently once you've used it. Someone who has trained for five years fights for 2% a year. You could take three minutes off your 5K by Christmas.

Goggins started at 297 pounds and couldn't run a mile. You can run twenty minutes. You are not starting from behind — you're starting from the start, which is where everybody starts.

Forget pace. Train by effort.

No pace zones until week 12, when you'll have real data

Every pace target is deleted for now. They were derived from a stale time trial and they were all too fast. Until week 12 you have exactly one control: the talk test.

Finding your easy pace on day one

  1. Set the belt to 6.5, incline 1%.
  2. After five minutes, say a full sentence out loud. Not a word — a sentence.
  3. Can't get through it? Drop 0.3 and try again in five minutes.
  4. Effortless, could hold it all day? Add 0.3.
  5. Whatever number you land on is your easy pace. Write it down. That's the number for the next six weeks.

Most people in your position land somewhere between 6.0 and 7.0. If yours is 6.0, that's fine. If it's 5.8, that's fine too — it just means walking briskly and jogging are close together right now, and that gap widens fast.

Run/walk is a tool, not a failure

If you can't hold conversational effort continuously, don't grind through it — break it up. 5 minutes running, 1 minute walking, repeat. The walk breaks let your heart rate settle so the running portions stay genuinely easy, which is the entire point. You accumulate more quality minutes this way than by running one hard block and needing three days to recover.

Drop the walk breaks when they start feeling unnecessary, not on a schedule.

What to expect, so you don't misread it

Your easy pace will move fast. It's normal to gain 1.0–1.5 km/h on your conversational speed within six weeks — the same effort that gives you 6.5 today gives you 8.0 by week 6. That's not you getting tougher. That's stroke volume, capillary density and mitochondria, and it's the fastest-moving adaptation in the sport.

Re-run the talk test every couple of weeks and let the belt speed follow it up. Don't force the number; let the effort set it.

What changes in your lifting

Keep, unchanged

  • Push and pull, merged onto Monday and Wednesday. Weighted dips, weighted pull-ups, loaded push-ups. Chase the +20 kg pull-up — running volume is low enough for months that your upper body barely notices.
  • The mobility block. Ankle dorsiflexion especially.
  • Core: ab wheel, suitcase carry, Pallof press.

Replace — the runner's legs circuit

Around 15 minutes, added to Monday and Wednesday, replacing Lower A and Lower B. At your current running volume this is doing more for your injury resistance than the running is.

How to use this table

Weeks 1–12 have no quality sessions at all beyond a few strides, and that is the entire point. Your engine needs volume at low effort far more than it needs hard intervals right now, and hard intervals on an undeveloped base are how beginners get hurt.

If a week feels too hard, repeat it rather than pushing on. This table is a route, not a timetable. Adding two weeks to the block costs you nothing. An eight-week injury costs you the block.

Test days

Two real tests. Everything before week 12 is feel.

WeekTestWhat it sets
4Talk test, repeatedNot a time trial — just re-find your conversational speed. It will have moved.
125K time trial, all outYour first real number. Every pace zone gets built from this.

Two hard tests in six months is deliberate. Testing costs two or three days of recovery, and adaptation is slow enough that measuring monthly gives you noise plus a reason to feel bad on a bad day.

The feedback you want more often is already there: the belt speed that passes your talk test. Same effort, rising speed, week after week. That's your fitness moving, and it costs nothing to read.

How to run the week-12 trial

  • Same treadmill every time. Belts drift out of calibration, so consistency matters more than accuracy.
  • Warm up 15 minutes easy plus 4 strides. Cold-starting a time trial costs you 20–30 seconds.
  • Even splits. The most common way to ruin one is the first kilometre.
  • Two easy days beforehand — that's why it sits on a down week.
  • Log the conditions. A bad number on four hours' sleep isn't a plateau.

Heat and the treadmill

You're building through a Gulf summer

  • August to October: treadmill by default. Controllable pace, forgiving surface, no heat-casualty risk. Incline 0.5–1%.
  • Never run outdoors 9am–6pm from June to September. If you go outside it's before 5:30am, with water.
  • Feels-like above 32°C, go inside. Heat costs 20–40 seconds per kilometre and wrecks the talk test — you'll be gasping at an effort that's aerobically easy, and you'll misread it as being unfit.
  • Hydration: 500–800 ml per hour with 500–1000 mg sodium per litre once runs pass an hour.
  • From November, move the long run outside. Treadmill fitness transfers; road durability doesn't, and your half marathon won't be indoors.

Long treadmill runs are mentally brutal. That's not a flaw in the plan — it's the part that builds whatever keeps you moving when it stops being fun. Most people won't do it.

Fuelling

Simpler than the internet makes it

  • Under 60 minutes: water. Nothing else needed. That's most of your first three months.
  • Over 75 minutes: 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour, starting around 40 minutes. Begin practising this on the week-13 long runs so race day isn't an experiment.
  • Before a long run: 1–2 g carbohydrate per kg bodyweight, two hours out. At 70 kg that's 70–140 g.
  • Shoes: rotate two pairs, replace at 600–800 km. Track the mileage — worn midsoles are a genuine injury contributor.
  • Sleep: seven to nine hours. It's a training variable, not a lifestyle suggestion, and it's the cheapest one you own.

Four versions. Each time I wrote one, you handed back a harder, truer number — the knee, then which run was recent, then this. Most people defend the flattering version of themselves right up until the injury proves them wrong. You dismantled it yourself, unprompted, three times.

That instinct is worth more than any session in this document. Talent is common and it plateaus. Being willing to look straight at where you actually are is rare, and it compounds for decades.

So: 6.5 on the belt, 1% incline, a full sentence out loud every five minutes. Twenty-five minutes. That's week one, day one, and it is going to feel like nothing.

Do it anyway. Nobody's coming.
