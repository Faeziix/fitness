<ul>
<li><strong>Under 60 minutes:</strong> water only.</li>
<li><strong>Over 75 minutes:</strong> start fuelling. Begin at 30–40 g of carbohydrate per hour from week 5, taking the first one at 30 minutes rather than when you feel empty.</li>
<li><strong>From week 9:</strong> build toward <strong>60–90 g/hour</strong> on every long run. Use a 2:1 glucose-to-fructose product — glucose transport saturates around 60 g/h, and fructose opens a second pathway that gets you past it.</li>
<li><strong>The gut is trainable and it needs training.</strong> Untrained, most people tolerate about 60 g/h. Two weeks of deliberate practice measurably improves absorption and reduces stomach trouble. The best predictor of race-day GI problems is not having rehearsed.</li>
<li><strong>Fluid:</strong> 400–800 ml/hour with 300–600 mg sodium per hour, more in heat.</li>
<li><strong>Before long runs:</strong> 1–2 g carbohydrate per kg bodyweight two hours out. At 70 kg that's 70–140 g.</li>
<li><strong>Use exactly what the race hands out</strong>, or carry your own for the whole race. Race day is not the day to discover a new gel.</li>
</ul>

<Note label="Heat, and where you live">
<p>August to October, default to the treadmill at 0.5–1% incline or run before 5:30am. Heat inflates effort at any given pace — the same speed that's easy in the gym is threshold outdoors in August. Trust the effort and let the pace collapse; the distance still counts. <strong>From November, move the long run and all MP work outdoors.</strong> Your race isn't on a treadmill, and pacing on a belt teaches you nothing about pacing yourself.</p>
</Note>
